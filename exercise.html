Q1. Consider the reaction A + B → C. Assume A and B cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions. Which statement is correct? (i) A, B, C are all compounds and only C has a fixed composition (ii) C is a compound, and A and B have a fixed composition (iii) A and B are compounds, and C has a fixed composition (iv) A and B are elements, C is a compound, and has a fixed composition.

Answer: (iv) A and B are elements, C is a compound and has a fixed composition.

Why: a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances is, by definition, an element — so A and B are elements. When two elements combine chemically, the product C is a compound, and compounds always have a fixed composition (their elements are in a fixed ratio, like H : O = 2 : 1 in water). Options (i) and (iii) wrongly call A and B compounds; option (ii) is vague and does not identify A and B as elements.

Q2. Assertion: Air is a mixture. Reason: A mixture is formed when two or more substances are mixed without undergoing any chemical change. Choose: (i) Both true, Reason is the correct explanation (ii) Both true, Reason not the correct explanation (iii) Assertion true, Reason false (iv) Assertion false, Reason true.

Answer: (i) Both Assertion and Reason are true, and the Reason is the correct explanation of the Assertion.

Why: air contains nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour simply mixed together — none of them has reacted with another; oxygen still supports burning, nitrogen still does not, and their proportions can vary. That is exactly the definition given in the Reason, so the Reason explains why air is a mixture.

Q3. Water, a compound, has different properties compared to those of the elements oxygen and hydrogen from which it is formed. Justify this statement.
An element's molecule has identical atoms; a compound's molecule has atoms of different elements joined chemically in a fixed ratio (water: two hydrogen, one oxygen).

Water is formed when hydrogen and oxygen combine chemically in a fixed ratio (H : O = 2 : 1). In a chemical combination the atoms are joined into a completely new kind of particle (the water molecule), and a new particle has its own properties:

HydrogenOxygenWater
Gas at room temperatureGas at room temperatureLiquid at room temperature
A fuel — burns with a popSupports combustionExtinguishes fire
Cannot be drunkNeeded for breathingEssential for life as a drink and solvent

This is the general rule for compounds: their properties differ from those of their constituent elements (compare sodium + chlorine → common salt; iron + sulfur → iron sulfide).

Q4. In which case are all the examples correctly matched? Give reasons. (i) Elements — water, nitrogen, iron, air (ii) Uniform mixtures — minerals, seawater, bronze, air (iii) Pure substances — carbon dioxide, iron, oxygen, sugar (iv) Non-uniform mixtures — air, sand, brass, muddy water.

Answer: (iii) Pure substances — carbon dioxide, iron, oxygen, sugar.

OptionVerdictReason
(i)IncorrectNitrogen and iron are elements, but water is a compound and air is a mixture.
(ii)IncorrectSeawater, bronze and air are uniform mixtures, but minerals are compounds (or elements), not mixtures.
(iii)CorrectCarbon dioxide and sugar are compounds; iron and oxygen are elements — all four are made of one kind of particle, i.e., pure substances.
(iv)IncorrectSand and muddy water are non-uniform, but air and brass are uniform mixtures.

Q8. Is it possible for a substance to be classified as both an element and a compound? Explain why or why not.

No. The two definitions exclude each other:

  • An element is made of only one kind of atom and cannot be broken down into simpler substances (oxygen, iron, gold).
  • A compound is made of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, and can be broken down into those elements by chemical means (water, iron sulfide).

A substance either contains one kind of atom or more than one — it cannot do both. So every pure substance is either an element or a compound, never both.

Q9. How would our daily lives change if water were not a compound but a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen?

If water were only a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gases, the two would keep their own properties, and life as we know it would be impossible:

  • No liquid water: both gases stay gases at room temperature, so no rivers, lakes, oceans, rain or clouds — no water cycle or stable climate.
  • Explosive danger: hydrogen is a fuel and oxygen supports combustion; a mixture of the two explodes with a spark. A "glass of water" would be a bomb, and nothing could be used to put out fires.
  • No solvent for life: digestion, transport of nutrients in blood and plants, and all cell chemistry depend on liquid water dissolving substances. A gas mixture cannot do this — no living things.
  • Separable: being a mixture, the gases could simply separate or diffuse away into the air.

Only because water is a compound — with new properties that neither element has — can it be the liquid that sustains life.

Q10. Analyse Fig. 8.24 (iron filings + dilute hydrochloric acid, gas collected in an inverted tube). Identify Gas A and write the word equation.

Gas A is hydrogen. Iron (a metal) reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid, displacing hydrogen from the acid. The gas is colourless and odourless and burns with a 'pop' sound when a burning splinter is brought near it.

(Compare: if the tube had iron sulfide instead, the gas would be hydrogen sulfide with a rotten-egg smell.)

Q12. How can gold be classified as both a mineral and a metal?

The two words describe two different things, so gold can be both:

  • As a mineral: gold is found in nature in rocks and river deposits as a natural, solid substance with a fixed chemical composition (pure gold). That is the definition of a mineral; since it is a pure element and not a compound, it is a native mineral.
  • As a metal: gold is an element whose atoms give it metallic properties — shiny lustre, malleability (can be beaten into thin sheets), ductility, and good conduction of heat and electricity. That makes it a metal.

In short: where it occurs in nature makes gold a mineral; what kind of substance it is makes gold a metal.
