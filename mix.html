Everything around us is matter — and mostly mixed

The staircase, the air, the water in your bottle, your lunch, your clothes, the trees — all of these are matter (anything that has mass and occupies space), made up of tiny particles. But most things around us are not made of just one substance; they are two or more substances mixed together.

Definition of a mixture

When two or more substances are mixed such that each substance retains its own properties, the result is called a mixture. The individual substances in it are called its components. The components do not react chemically with each other — sugar in water is still sugar and water; the sugar still tastes sweet and can be recovered by evaporating the water.

Why is "retains its properties" the key idea? Because that is exactly what separates a mixture from a compound. In a mixture nothing new is formed: poha still contains flattened rice, peanuts and onion, each tasting as it does alone. If a new substance with new properties formed, it would be a chemical change, not mixing.

Non-uniform and uniform mixtures

Left: components (green gram, chickpea, onion, tomato) are visible and unevenly spread. Right: sugar particles (pink) sit evenly between water particles (blue) — you cannot see them apart even with a microscope.
  • Non-uniform mixture — the components are visible with the naked eye or a magnifying device and are unevenly spread. Examples: sprout salad (green gram, chickpeas, onion, tomato), poha, sand and water, muddy water, iron filings and sulfur powder.
  • Uniform mixture — the components are evenly distributed and cannot be seen separately, not even with a microscope. Examples: sugar in water, lemonade, soup, seawater, air, and alloys.
How can sugar "disappear" in water yet be a mixture? Sugar breaks up into its constituent particles which slip into the spaces between water particles (Chapter 7). The particles are far too small to see, but every part of the solution tastes equally sweet — evenly mixed, and still just sugar plus water.

Alloys — uniform mixtures of metals

Stainless steel is a mixture of iron, nickel, chromium and a little carbon, mixed so uniformly that the whole looks the same everywhere. Such uniform mixtures of metals (or a metal and a non-metal) are called alloys. Brass = copper + zinc; bronze = copper + tin.

Our scientific heritage: ancient Indian texts (Charaka Samhita, Susruta Samhita, Rasaratna Samucchaya, Rasa Jala Nidhi) call such mixtures Mishraloha. Bronze (Kamsya) was made from 4 parts copper (Tamra) and 1 part tin (Vanga) and used for utensils believed to improve digestion and immunity.

Why is oil-and-water non-uniform but vinegar uniform? Acetic acid dissolves completely in water, so its particles spread evenly. Oil does not dissolve — it floats as a separate visible layer, so the two components are seen apart.

Mixtures in science vs everyday talk

In everyday language, poha is a "mixture" of other mixtures. In science, the components of a mixture are always pure substances (e.g., sugar, salt, water). Also, in daily life we separate a mixture to keep the useful part and throw away the rest, but in science, separation is done to obtain pure substances.

❓ Frequently asked questions

What is a mixture?

Two or more substances mixed together such that each retains its own properties and none reacts chemically with the other. Its individual substances are called components.

How do I tell a uniform mixture from a non-uniform one?

If you can see the components separately (by eye or magnifier) and they are unevenly spread, it is non-uniform (salad, muddy water). If the components are evenly spread and cannot be distinguished even with a microscope, it is uniform (sugar solution, air, alloys).

🎧 Listen (Hinglish explanation)
Why are alloys called mixtures and not compounds?

The metals in an alloy are melted and mixed, not chemically combined in a fixed ratio; proportions can be varied to change properties, and each metal keeps its nature.

🔊
🎧 Listen (Hinglish explanation)
Is sugar dissolved in water a mixture?

Yes — a uniform mixture. Sugar and water keep their properties; the water can be evaporated to get the sugar back.

🎧 Listen (Hinglish explanation)
What does 'mixture' mean in science compared with everyday use?

In science all components must be pure substances, and mixtures are separated to obtain pure substances — not just to keep a useful part.
